**What it is.** How free the commanded capital actually is — whose money it
is, and what binds it. This is the correction that keeps
[commanded capital](/metric/commanded-capital/) honest: gross command
overstates power unless you say how much of it is encumbered.

**The bands**

| band | meaning | archetype |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 🟢 **free** | the actor's own, unencumbered — deployable at will | Apple, Nvidia, Alphabet |
| 🟡 **mixed** | deployable, but partly bound — commitments, partners, thin cushions | TSMC, the frontier labs |
| 🟠 **constrained** | debt, covenants, or a controlling parent set the terms | Oracle, Intel, Arm (87% SoftBank) |
| 🔴 **locked** | client, fiduciary, policyholder, or state money — not redirectable | BlackRock, Vanguard, China Life |

**How we assign it.** A judgment call with a cited rationale, per actor: read
the balance sheet's encumbrances (debt load, covenants), the ownership
structure (parent control, state mandate), and the legal character of the
capital (fiduciary duty, policyholder liabilities). Each band claim carries
its reasoning; changes go through the steering loop like any board edit.

**What it is deliberately NOT.** Optionality is **never derived as
thrust ÷ weight**. That ratio measures deployment intensity, and real-options
theory (Dixit–Pindyck) runs the other way: irreversible commitment *destroys*
option value, so high thrust can mean *less* freedom, not more. The
decisive cases prove the independence — Apple is free but nearly idle
(could deploy, returns ~$94B/yr instead); BlackRock is locked despite $14T
(client money); OpenAI deploys ferociously against capital that is already
spoken for. A ratio would collapse all three into noise. Measured
encumbrance keeps them apart.

**Prior art.** Beckley's net-power correction — gross resources must be
deflated by the cost and constraint of holding them — applied as a visible
band instead of a hidden multiplier.
